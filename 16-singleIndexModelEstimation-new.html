16.3 Estimation of SI Model

Consider the SI model Equation 15.1 - Equation 15.5. The asset specific parameters to be estimated are \(\beta_{i,0}\), \(\beta_{i,M}\) and \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\), \((i=1,\ldots,N)\), and the market parameters to be estimated are \(\mu_{M}\) and \(\sigma_{M}^{2}\). These parameters can be estimated using the plug-in principle, linear regression, and maximum likelihood. All methods give essentially the same estimators for the SI model parameters.

16.3.1 Plug-in principle estimates

Let \(\{(r_{it},r_{Mt})\}_{t=1}^{T}\) denote a sample of size \(T\) of observed returns on asset \(i\) and the market return which are assumed to be generated from the SI model. Equation 15.1 - Equation 15.17. Recall, the plug-in principle says to estimate population model parameters using appropriate sample statistics. For the market parameters, the plug-in principle estimates are the same as the GWN model estimates \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\mu}_{M} & = & \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt},\\ \hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2} & = & \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M})^{2}. \end{eqnarray*}\] From Equation 15.10 - Equation 15.6 we see that \(\beta_{i,0}\) and \(\beta_{i,M}\) are functions of population parameters \[\begin{eqnarray*} \beta_{i,0} & = & \mu_{i}-\beta_{i,M}\mu_{M},\\ \beta_{i,M} & = & \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{it},R_{Mt})}{\mathrm{var}(R_{Mt})}=\frac{\sigma_{iM}}{\sigma_{M}^{2}}. \end{eqnarray*}\] The corresponding plug-in principle estimates are then: \[ \hat{\beta}_{i,0} = \hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\hat{\mu}_{M}, \tag{16.1}\] \[ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{iM}}{\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}}, \tag{16.2}\] where \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\mu}_{i} & = & \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it},\\ \hat{\sigma}_{iM} & = & \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{it}-\hat{\mu}_{i})(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M}). \end{eqnarray*}\]

Given the plug-in principle estimates \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\), the plug-in principle estimate of \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is \[ \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}=r_{it}-\hat{\beta}_{i,0}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}r_{Mt},\,t=1,\ldots,T. \tag{16.3}\] Using Equation 16.3, the plug-in principle estimate of \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) is the sample variance of \(\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}\}_{t=1}^{T}\) (adjusted for the number of degrees of freedom): \[ \hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2} =\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}^{2}=\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{it}-\hat{\beta}_{i,0}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}r_{Mt}\right)^{2}. \tag{16.4}\] Plug-in principle estimates of \(R^{2}\) based on Equation 15.19 can be computed using \[ \hat{R}^{2} =\frac{\hat{\beta}_{i}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}=1-\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}. \tag{16.5}\]

Example 16.1 (Computing plug-in principle estimators for SI model parameters) Consider computing the plug-in principle estimates for \(\beta_{i,0},\) \(\beta_{i,M}\) and \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) from the example data using the formulas Equation 16.1, Equation 16.2 and Equation 16.4, respectively. First, extract the sample statistics \(\hat{\mu}_{i}\), \(\hat{\sigma}_{iM}\), \(\hat{\mu}_{M}\), and \(\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}\):

Code 
assetNames = colnames(siRetS)[1:4] 
muhat = colMeans(siRetS) 
Sigmahat = var(siRetS)
sig2hat = diag(Sigmahat)
covAssetsSp500 = Sigmahat[assetNames, "SP500"]

Next, estimate \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) using

Code 
betaMHat = covAssetsSp500/sig2hat["SP500"]
betaMHat
#>    BA   JWN  MSFT  SBUX 
#> 0.978 1.485 1.303 1.057

Here, we see that \(\hat{\beta}_{BA,M}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{SBUX,M}\) are very close to one and that \(\hat{\beta}_{JWN,M}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{MSFT,M}\) are slightly bigger than one. Using the estimates of \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) and the sample statistics \(\hat{\mu}_{i}\) and \(\hat{\mu}_{M}\) the estimates for \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) are

Code 
beta0Hat = muhat[assetNames] - betaMHat*muhat["SP500"] 
beta0Hat
#>      BA     JWN    MSFT    SBUX 
#> 0.00516 0.01231 0.00544 0.01785

All of the estimates of \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) are close to zero.

The estimates of \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) and \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}\) can be computed using:

Code 
sig2eHat = rep(0, length(assetNames)) 
names(sig2eHat) = assetNames 
for (aName in assetNames) {   
     eHat = siRetS[, aName] - beta0Hat[aName] - betaMHat[aName]*siRetS[, "SP500"]   
     sig2eHat[aName] = crossprod(eHat)/(length(eHat) - 2) 
} 
sigeHat = sqrt(sig2eHat)
ans = rbind(sig2eHat,sigeHat)
ans
#>               BA     JWN    MSFT    SBUX

#> sigeHat  0.07624 0.09971 0.08036 0.09703

Here, Nordstrom (JWN) has the largest asset specific volatility and Boeing (BA) has the lowest.

Lastly, the estimates of \(R^{2}\) can be computed using

Code 
R2 = 1 - sig2eHat/sig2hat[assetNames] 
R2
#>    BA   JWN  MSFT  SBUX 
#> 0.270 0.334 0.373 0.210

Microsoft has the largest fraction of returns explained by the market index and Starbucks has the lowest.

16.3.2 Least squares estimates

The SI model representation Equation 15.1 shows that returns are a linear function of the market return and an asset specific error term \[ R_{it}=\beta_{i,0}+\beta_{i,M}R_{Mt}+\varepsilon_{it}, \] here \(\beta_{i,0}\) is the intercept and \(\beta_{i,M}\) is the slope. Least squares regression is a method for estimating \(\beta_{i,0}\) and \(\beta_{i,M}\) by finding the “best fitting” line to the scatterplot of returns where \(R_{it}\) is on the vertical axis and \(R_{Mt}\) is on the horizontal axis.

To see how the method of least squares determines the “best fitting” line, consider the scatterplot of the sample returns on Boeing and the S&P 500 index illustrated in Figure xxx. In the figure, the black line is a fitted line with initial guess \(\hat{\beta}_{BA,0}=0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{BA,M}=0.5.\) The differences between the observed returns (blue dots) and the values on the fitted line are the estimated errors \(\hat{\varepsilon}_{BA,t}=r_{BA,t}-\hat{\beta}_{BA,0}-\hat{\beta}_{BA,M}r_{Mt}=r_{BA,t}-0-0.5\times R_{Mt}.\) Some estimated errors are big and some are small. The overall fit of the line can be measured using a statistic based on all \(t=1,\ldots,T\) of the estimated errors. A natural choice is the sum of the errors \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}\). However, this choice can be misleading due to the canceling out of large positive and negative errors. To avoid this problem, it is better to measure the overall fit using \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{|\varepsilon}_{t}|\) or \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}^{2}.\) Then the best fitting line can be determined by finding the intercept and slope values that minimize \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{|\varepsilon}_{t}|\) or \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}^{2}.\)

The method of least squares regression defines the “best fitting” line by finding the intercept and slope values that minimize the sum of squared errors \[ \mathrm{SSE}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0},\hat{\beta}_{i,M})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{it}-\hat{\beta}_{i,0}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}r_{Mt}\right)^{2}. \tag{16.6}\] Because \(\mathrm{SSE}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0},\hat{\beta}_{i,M})\) is a continuous and differential function of \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\), the minimizing values of \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) can be determined using simple calculus. The first order conditions for a minimum are: \[ 0 =\frac{\partial\mathrm{SSE}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0},\hat{\beta}_{i,M})}{\partial\hat{\beta}_{i,0}}=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{it}-\hat{\beta}_{i,0}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}r_{Mt})=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}, \tag{16.7}\] \[ 0 =\frac{\partial\mathrm{SSE}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0},\hat{\beta}_{i,M})}{\partial\hat{\beta}_{i,M}}=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{it}-\hat{\beta}_{i,0}-\hat{\beta}_{i,M}r_{Mt})r_{Mt}=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}r_{Mt}. \tag{16.8}\] These are two linear equations in two unknowns which can be re-expressed as \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\beta}_{i,0}T+\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt} & = & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it},\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,0}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}+\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2} & = & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt}. \end{eqnarray*}\] Using matrix algebra, we can write these equations as: \[ \left(\begin{array}{cc} T & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt} & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2} \end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c} \hat{\beta}_{i,0}\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c} \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}\\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt} \end{array}\right), \tag{16.9}\] which is of the form \(\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}\) with \[ \mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}{cc} T & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt} & \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2} \end{array}\right),\,\mathbf{x}=\left(\begin{array}{c} \hat{\beta}_{i,0}\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} \end{array}\right),\,\mathbf{b}=\left(\begin{array}{c} \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}\\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt} \end{array}\right). \] Hence, we can determine \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) by solving \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}.\) Now,1 \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathbf{A}^{-1} & = & \frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(\mathbf{A})}\left(\begin{array}{cc} \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2} & -\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\\ -\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt} & T \end{array}\right),\\ \mathrm{det}(\mathbf{A}) & = & T\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2}-\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\right)^{2}=T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M}\right)^{2},\\ \hat{\mu}_{M} & = & \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}. \end{eqnarray*}\] Consequently, \[ \left(\begin{array}{c} \hat{\beta}_{i,0}\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} \end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M}\right)^{2}}\left(\begin{array}{cc} \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2} & -\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\\ -\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt} & T \end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c} \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}\\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt} \end{array}\right) \tag{16.10}\] and so \[ \hat{\beta}_{i,0} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt}}{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M}\right)^{2}}, \tag{16.11}\] \[ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} = \frac{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}r_{Mt}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it}}{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{Mt}-\hat{\mu}_{M}\right)^{2}}. \tag{16.12}\] After a little bit of algebra (see end-of-chapter exercises) it can be shown that \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\beta}_{i,0} & = & \hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{i}\hat{\mu}_{M},\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} & = & \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{iM}}{\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}}, \end{eqnarray*}\] which are plug-in estimates for \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) determined earlier. Hence, the least squares estimates of \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) are identical to the plug-in estimates.

The solution for the least squares estimates in Equation 16.10 has an elegant representation using matrix algebra. To see this, define the \(T\times1\) vectors \(\mathbf{r}_{i}=(r_{i1},\ldots,r_{iT})^{\prime}\), \(\mathbf{r}_{M}=(r_{M1},\ldots,r_{MT})^{\prime}\) and \(\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\prime}\). Then we can re-write Equation 16.9 as \[ \left(\begin{array}{cc} \mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{M}\\ \mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{M} & \mathbf{r}_{M}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{M} \end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c} \hat{\beta}_{i,0}\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{i}\\ \mathbf{r}_{M}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{i} \end{array}\right) \] or \[ \mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}=\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{i} \tag{16.13}\] where \(\mathbf{X}=(\begin{array}{cc} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{r}_{M})\end{array}\)is a \(T\times2\) matrix and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}=(\hat{\beta}_{i,0},\hat{\beta}_{i,M})^{\prime}\). Provided \(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}\) is invertible, solving Equation 16.13 for \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}\) gives the least squares estimates in matrix form: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{r}_{i}. \tag{16.14}\] The matrix form solution Equation 16.14 is especially convenient for computation in R.

The least squares estimates of \(\varepsilon_{t}\), \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) and \(R^{2}\) are the same as the plug-in estimators Equation 16.3, Equation 16.4 and Equation 16.5, respectively. In the context of least squares estimation, the estimate \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}=\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}}\) is called the standard error of the regression and measures the typical magnitude of \(\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}\) (difference between observed return and fitted regression line).

16.4 Statistical Properties of SI Model Estimates

To determine the statistical properties of the plug-in principle/least squares/ML estimators \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\), \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) in the SI model, we treat them as functions of the random variables \(\{(R_{i,t},R_{Mt})\}_{t=1}^{T}\) where \(R_{t}\) and \(R_{Mt}\) are assumed to be generated by the SI model Equation 15.1 - Equation 15.5.

16.4.1 Bias

In the SI model, the estimators \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\), \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) (with degrees-of-freedom adjustment) are unbiased: \[\begin{eqnarray*} E[\hat{\beta}_{i,0}] & = & \beta_{i,0},\\ E[\hat{\beta}_{i,M}] & = & \beta_{i,M},\\ E[\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}] & = & \sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}. \end{eqnarray*}\] To shows that \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) are unbiased, it is useful to consider the SI model for asset \(i\) in matrix form for \(t=1,\ldots,T\): \[ \mathbf{R}_{i}=\beta_{i,0}\mathbf{1}+\beta_{i,M}\mathbf{R}_{M}+\varepsilon_{i}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}, \] where \(\mathbf{R}_{i}=(R_{i1},\ldots,R_{iT})^{\prime},\) \(\mathbf{R}_{M}=(R_{M1},\ldots,R_{MT})^{\prime}\), \(\varepsilon_{i}=(\varepsilon_{i1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{iT})^{\prime}\), \(\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\prime}\), \(\mathbf{X}=(\begin{array}{cc} \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{R}_{M})\end{array},\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}=(\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,M})^{\prime}.\) The estimator for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\) is \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i} & = & (\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}_{i}. \end{eqnarray*}\] Pluging in \(\mathbf{R}_{i}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\) gives \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i} & = & (\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{X}\gamma_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\right)=(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}\gamma_{i}+(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}\\ & = & \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}+(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}. \end{eqnarray*}\] Then \[\begin{eqnarray*} E[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}] & = & \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}+E\left[(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}\right]\\ & = & \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}+E\left[(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\right]E[\varepsilon_{i}]\,(\mathrm{because}\,\varepsilon_{it}\,\mathrm{is}\,\mathrm{independent}\,\mathrm{of}\,R_{Mt})\\ & = & \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\,(\mathrm{because}\,E[\varepsilon_{i}]=\mathbf{0}). \end{eqnarray*}\] The derivation of \(E[\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}]=\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\) is beyond the scope of this book and can be found in graduate econometrics textbooks such as Hayashi (1980).

16.4.2 Precision

Under the assumptions of the SI model, analytic formulas for estimates of the standard errors for \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) are given by: \[ \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}) \approx\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}}{\sqrt{T\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{Mt}^{2}}, \tag{16.15}\] \[ \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}) \approx\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}}{\sqrt{T\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}}}, \tag{16.16}\] where “\(\approx\)” denotes an approximation based on the CLT that gets more accurate the larger the sample size.

Remarks:

  • \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0})\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M})\) are smaller the smaller is \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}\). That is, the closer are returns to the fitted regression line the smaller are the estimation errors in \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\).
  • \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M})\) is smaller the larger is \(\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}\). That is, the greater the variability in the market return \(R_{Mt}\) the smaller is the estimation error in the estimated slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\). This is illustrated in Figure xxx. The left panel shows a data sample from the SI model with a small value of \(\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}\) and the right panel shows a sample with a large value of \(\hat{\sigma}_{M}^{2}\). The right panel shows that the high variability in \(R_{Mt}\) makes it easier to identify the slope of the line.
  • Both \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0})\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M})\) go to zero as the sample size, \(T\), gets large. Since \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) are unbiased estimators, this implies that they are also consistent estimators. That is, they converge to the true values \(\beta_{i,0}\) and \(\beta_{i,M}\), respectively, as \(T\rightarrow\infty\).
  • In R, the standard error values Equation 16.15 and Equation 16.16 are computed using the summary() function on an “lm” object.

There are no easy formulas for the estimated standard errors for \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}^{2}\), \(\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}\) and \(\hat{R}^{2}\). Estimated standard errors for these estimators, however, can be easily computed using the bootstrap.

16.4.3 Sampling distribution and confidence intervals.

Using arguments based on the CLT, it can be shown that for large enough \(T\) the estimators \(\hat{\beta}_{i,0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{i,M}\) are approximately normally distributed: \[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\beta}_{i,0} & \sim & N(\beta_{i,0},\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0})^{2}),\\ \hat{\beta}_{i,M} & \sim & N(\beta_{i,M},\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M})^{2}), \end{eqnarray*}\] where \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,0})\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat{\beta}_{i,M})\) are given by Equation 16.15 and Equation 16.16, respectively.
