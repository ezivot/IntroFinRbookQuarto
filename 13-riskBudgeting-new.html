13  Portfolio Risk Budgeting

Updated: September 13, 2026

Copyright © Eric Zivot 2026

The idea of portfolio risk budgeting is to decompose a measure of portfolio risk into risk contributions from the individual assets in the portfolio. In this way, an investor can see which assets are most responsible for portfolio risk and can then make informed decisions, if necessary, about re-balancing the portfolio to alter the risk. Portfolio risk budgeting reports, which summarize asset contributions to portfolio risk, are becoming standard in industry practice. In addition, it has become popular to consider portfolio construction using risk budgeting to create so-called “risk-parity” portfolios. In risk-parity portfolios, portfolio weights are constructed so that that each asset has equal risk contributions to portfolio risk.1

I first motivate risk budgeting using the portfolio return variance and standard deviation as measures of portfolio risk. I then show how Euler’s theorem can be used to do risk budgeting for portfolio risk measures that are homogeneous of degree one in the portfolio weights. Such risk measures include portfolio return standard deviation (volatility) and Value-at-Risk for general return distributions.

13.1 Risk Budgeting Using Portfolio Variance and Portfolio Standard Deviation

I motivate portfolio risk budgeting in the simple context of a two risky asset portfolio. To illustrate, consider forming a portfolio consisting of two risky assets (asset 1 and asset 2) with portfolio shares \(x_{1}\) and \(x_{2}\) such that \(x_{1}+x_{2}=1.\) I assume that the GWN model holds for the simple returns on each asset. The portfolio return is given by \(R_{p}=x_{1}R_{1}+x_{2}R_{2}\), and the portfolio expected return and variance are given by \(\mu_{p}=x_{1}\mu_{1}+x_{2}\mu_{2}\) and \(\sigma_{p}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\), respectively. The portfolio variance, \(\sigma_{p}^{2},\) and standard deviation, \(\sigma_{p},\) are natural measures of portfolio risk. The advantage of using \(\sigma_{p}\) is that it is in the same units as the portfolio return. Risk budgeting concerns the following question: what are the contributions of assets 1 and 2 to portfolio risk captured by \(\sigma_{p}^{2}\) or \(\sigma_{p}\)?

13.1.1 Case 1: uncorrelated assets

To answer this question, I consider first the formula for portfolio variance. If the asset returns are uncorrelated (\(\sigma_{12}=0)\) then \[ \sigma_{p}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2} \] gives an additive decomposition of portfolio risk: \(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\) is the variance contribution from asset 1, and \(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}\) is the variance contribution from asset 2. Clearly, asset 1 has a higher contribution than asset 2 if \(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}>x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}\). For example, in an equally weighted portfolio asset 1’s risk contribution is higher than asset 2’s contribution if asset 1’s return is more variable than asset 2’s return. I can also define asset percent contributions to risk, which divide the asset contributions by portfolio variance, as \(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{p}^{2}\) and \(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{p}^{2},\) respectively. The percent contributions have the property that they add to \(100\%.\) Notice that an equally weighted portfolio may not have equal contributions to risk.

If I measure risk by portfolio volatility, \(\sigma_{p}=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}\), I may be tempted to define the contributions of assets 1 and 2 to risk as \(x_{1}\sigma_{1}\) and \(x_{2}\sigma_{2},\) respectively. However, this would not be correct because I would not get an additive decomposition \[ \sigma_{p}=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}\neq x_{1}\sigma_{1}+x_{2}\sigma_{2}. \] To get an additive decomposition I have to define the risk contributions as \(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{p}\) and \(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{p},\) respectively. Then \[ \frac{x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{p}}+\frac{x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{p}}=\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}}{\sigma_{p}}=\sigma_{p}. \]

Notice that the percent contributions to portfolio volatility are the same as the percent contributions to portfolio variance.

13.1.2 Case 2: correlated assets

If the asset returns are correlated (\(\sigma_{12}\neq0\)) then the risk decomposition becomes a bit more complicated because I have to decide what to do with the covariance contribution to portfolio variance. However, the formula for \(\sigma_{p}^{2}\) suggests a simple additive decomposition: \[\begin{align*} \sigma_{p}^{2} & =x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\\ & =\left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)+\left(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)\\ & =\left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\rho_{12}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\right)+\left(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\rho_{12}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\right) \end{align*}\] Now, \(\left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)\) and \(\left(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)\) are portfolio variance contributions of assets 1 and 2, respectively. Here, I split the covariance contribution to portfolio variance, \(2x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\), evenly between the two assets. Now, the correlation between the two asset returns also plays a role in the risk decomposition. The additive decomposition for \(\sigma_{p}\) is \[ \sigma_{p}=\frac{x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{p}}+\frac{x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{p}}, \] which gives \(\left(\frac{x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{p}}\right)\) and \(\left(\frac{x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{p}}\right)\) as the asset contributions to portfolio volatility, respectively.

13.1.3 The general case of \(N\) assets

The risk budgeting decompositions I presented above for two asset portfolios extend naturally to general \(N\) asset portfolios. In the case where all assets are mutually uncorrelated, asset i’s contributions to portfolio variance and volatility are \(x_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\) and \(x_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{p}\), respectively. In the case of correlated asset returns, all pairwise covariances enter into asset i’s contributions. The portfolio variance contribution is \[ x_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{i}x_{j}\sigma_{ij} \] and the portfolio volatility contribution is \[ \left(x_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{i}x_{j}\sigma_{ij}\right)/\sigma_{p} \]. As you might expect, these formulae can be simplified using matrix algebra.

13.2 Euler’s Theorem and Risk Decompositions

When I used \(\sigma_{p}^{2}\) or \(\sigma_{p}\) to measure portfolio risk, I was able to easily derive sensible risk decompositions in the two risky asset case. However, if I measure portfolio risk by value-at-risk or some other risk measure it is not so obvious how to define individual asset risk contributions. For portfolio risk measures that are homogenous functions of degree one in the portfolio weights, Euler’s theorem provides a general method for decomposing risk into asset specific contributions.

13.2.1 Homogenous functions of degree one

First I define a homogenous function of degree one.

Definition 13.1 (homogenous function of degree one) Let \(f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})\) be a continuous and differentiable function of the variables \(x_{1},\,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\). The function \(f\) is homogeneous of degree one if for any constant \(c > 0,\) \(f(cx_{1},cx_{2},\ldots,cx_{N})=cf(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}).\) In matrix notation I have \(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=f(\mathbf{x})\) where \(\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{\prime}\). Then \(f\) is homogeneous of degree one if \(f(c\cdot\mathbf{x})=c\cdot f(\mathbf{x})\).

Example 13.1 (Homogenous functions of degree one) Consider first the function \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+x_{2}.\) Then \[ f(cx_{1},cx_{2})=cx_{1}+cx_{2}=c(x_{1}+x_{2})=cf(x_{1},x_{2}) \] so that \(x_{1}+x_{2}\) is homogenous of degree one. Next, let \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}.\) Then \[ f(cx_{1},cx_{2})=c^{2}x_{1}^{2}+c^{2}x_{2}^{2}=c^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\neq cf(x_{1},x_{2}) \] so that \(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\) is not homogenous of degree one. Finally, let \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\) Then \[ f(cx_{1},cx_{2})=\sqrt{c^{2}x_{1}^{2}+c^{2}x_{2}^{2}}=c\sqrt{(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})}=cf(x_{1},x_{2}) \] so that \(\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\) is homogenous of degree one.

In matrix notation, define \(\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\prime}\) and \(\mathbf{1}=(1,1)^{\prime}\). Let \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+x_{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{1=f(\mathbf{x})}\). Then \[ f(c\cdot\mathbf{x})=\left(c\cdot\mathbf{x}\right)^{\prime}\mathbf{1}=c\cdot\mathbf{(x}^{\prime}1)=c\cdot f(\mathbf{x}). \] Let \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}=f\mathbf{(x)}\). Then \[ f(c\cdot\mathbf{x})=(c\cdot\mathbf{x})^{\prime}(c\cdot\mathbf{x})=c^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\neq c\cdot f(\mathbf{x}). \] Let \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x})^{1/2}=f(\mathbf{x})\). Then \[ f(c\cdot\mathbf{x})=\left((c\cdot\mathbf{x})^{\prime}(c\cdot\mathbf{x})\right)^{1/2}=c\cdot\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}=c\cdot f(\mathbf{x}). \] \(\blacksquare\)

Consider a portfolio of \(N\) assets with weight vector \(\mathbf{x}\), return vector \(\mathbf{R}\), expected return vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The portfolio return, expected return, variance, and volatility, are functions of the portfolio weight vector \(\mathbf{x}\): \[ \begin{aligned} R_{p}& =R_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{R} \\ \mu_{p} & =\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mu},\\ \sigma_{p}^{2} & =\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x},\\ \sigma_{p} & =\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{x})^{1/2}. \end{aligned} \] As a result, the portfolio return quantile and portfolio value-at-risk are also functions of the portfolio weight vector \(\mathbf{x}\): \[ \begin{aligned} q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(x) &=\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})+\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})q_{\alpha}^{Z}, \\ \mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x}) & = -q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(\mathbf{x})W_{0}. \end{aligned} \]

Proposition 13.1 (Portfolio quantites are homogenous of degree one in \(\mathbf{x}\)) The portfolio quantities \(R_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), \(\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), the normal quantile \(q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(x)\), and the normal value-at-risk \(\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\) are homogenous functions of degree one in the portfolio weight vector \(\mathbf{x}\).

The result for \(R_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) and \(\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) is trivial since they are linear functions of \(\mathbf{x}\). For example, \(R_{p}(c\mathbf{x})=(c\mathbf{x})^{\prime}\mathbf{R}=c\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}\right)=cR_{p}(\mathbf{x})\). The result for \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) is straightforward to show: \[\begin{align*} \sigma_{p}(c\cdot\mathbf{x}) & =((c\cdot\mathbf{x)}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{(}c\cdot\mathbf{x)})^{1/2}\\ & =c\cdot(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{1/2}\\ & =c\cdot\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}). \end{align*}\] The result for \(q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(\mathbf{x})\) follows because it is a linear function of \(\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) and \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\). The result for \(\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\) follows from the linear homogeneity of the normal return quantile.

In the proposition, I stated that \(q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(x)\) is homogenous of degree one in \(\mathbf{x}\) when returns are normally distributed. It turns out that the homogeneity of \(q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(x)\) holds generally for continuous distributions and even for the empirical quantile.

13.2.2 Euler’s theorem

Euler’s theorem gives an additive decomposition of a homogenous function of degree one.

Theorem 13.1 (Euler’s theorem) Let \(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=f(\mathbf{x})\) be a continuous, differentiable and homogenous of degree one function of the variables \(\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{\prime}\). Then, \[\begin{align*} f(\mathbf{x}) & =x_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}+x_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}+\cdots+x_{N}\cdot\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{N}}\\ & =\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}, \end{align*}\] where, \[ \underset{(N\times1)}{\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}}=\left(\begin{array}{c} \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}\\ \vdots\\ \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{N}} \end{array}\right). \]

Example 13.2 (Verifying Euler’s theorem) The function \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+x_{2}=f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{1}\) is homogenous of degree one, and: \[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}} & =\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}=1,\\ \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}} & =\left(\begin{array}{c} \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}\\ \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}} \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c} 1\\ 1 \end{array}\right)=\mathbf{1}. \end{align*}\] By Euler’s theorem, \[\begin{align*} f(x) & =x_{1}\cdot1+x_{2}\cdot1=x_{1}+x_{2}\\ & =\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{1}. \end{align*}\]

The function \(f(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{1/2}=f(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x})^{1/2}\) is homogenous of degree one, and: \[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}} & =\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}2x_{1}=x_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2},\\ \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}} & =\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}2x_{2}=x_{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}. \end{align*}\] By Euler’s theorem, \[\begin{align*} f(\mathbf{x}) & =x_{1}\cdot x_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}+x_{2}\cdot x_{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\\ & =\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\\ & =\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}. \end{align*}\]

Using matrix algebra, I have: \[ \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\partial\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1/2}2\mathbf{x}=\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1/2}. \] Then by Euler’s theorem: \[ f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1/2}=\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}. \]

\(\blacksquare\)

13.2.3 Risk decomposition using Euler’s theorem

Proposition 13.2 (Additive risk decomposition for homogenous of degree one portfolio risk measures) Let \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) denote a portfolio risk measure that is a homogenous function of degree one in the portfolio weight vector \(\mathbf{x}\). For example, \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) or \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x}).\) Then, Euler’s theorem gives the additive risk decomposition \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x}) & = x_{1}\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}+x_{2}\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}+\cdots+x_{N}\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{N}}\\ & = \sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}} \\ & = \mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}. \end{aligned} \]

The partial derivatives in ?eq-RiskBudgetRiskDecomposition are called asset marginal contributions to risk (MCRs): \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{RM} =\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}=\text{ marginal contribution of asset } i \tag{13.1}\]

The asset contributions to risk (CRs) are defined as the weighted marginal contributions: \[ \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{RM} =x_{i}\cdot\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{RM}=\text{ contribution of asset } i \tag{13.2}\] Then I can re-express the decomposition ?eq-RiskBudgetRiskDecomposition as \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x}) & = x_{1}\cdot\mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{RM}+x_{2}\cdot\mathrm{MCR}_{2}^{RM}+\cdots+x_{N}\cdot\mathrm{MCR}_{N}^{RM}\\ & = \mathrm{CR}_{1}^{RM}+\mathrm{CR}_{2}^{RM}+\cdots+\mathrm{CR}_{N}^{RM}\nonumber \end{aligned} \tag{13.3}\] If I divide both sides of Equation 13.3 by \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) I get the asset percent contributions to risk (PCRs) \[ \begin{aligned} 1 & = \frac{\mathrm{CR}_{1}^{RM}}{\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}+\frac{\mathrm{CR}_{2}^{RM}}{\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}+\cdots+\frac{\mathrm{CR}_{N}^{RM}}{\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}\\ & = \mathrm{PCR}_{1}^{RM}+\mathrm{PCR}_{2}^{RM}+\cdots+\mathrm{PCR}_{N}^{RM} \end{aligned} \] where \[ \mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{RM} =\frac{\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{RM}}{\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\text{ percent contribution of asset } i \tag{13.4}\] By construction the asset PCRs sum to one.

13.2.3.1 Risk decomposition using \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\)

Let \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{1/2}\). Because \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) is homogenous of degree 1 in \(\mathbf{x},\) by Euler’s theorem \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}) &= x_{1}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}+x_{2}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}+\cdots+x_{n}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{n}}\\ &=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}. \end{aligned} \tag{13.5}\] Now, \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}} & = \frac{\partial(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{1/2}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{-1/2}2\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\nonumber \\ & = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{1/2}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}. \end{aligned} \tag{13.6}\] Then, \[ \frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}=\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\text{i-th row of }\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}, \tag{13.7}\] and \[ \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma} = x_{i}\times\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}, \tag{13.8}\] \[ \mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma} = x_{i}\times\frac{\left(\Sigma \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}. \tag{13.9}\] Notice that: \[ \sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}). \]

Example 13.3 (Two asset example) For a two asset portfolio you have: \[\begin{eqnarray*} \sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x})^{1/2} & = & \left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)^{1/2},\\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x} & \mathbf{=} & \left(\begin{array}{cc} \sigma_{1}^{2} & \sigma_{12}\\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_{2}^{2} \end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c} x_{1}\\ x_{2} \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c} x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}\\ x_{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}\sigma_{12} \end{array}\right),\\ \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})} & = & \left(\begin{array}{c} \left(x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\\ \left(x_{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}) \end{array}\right), \end{eqnarray*}\] so that \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{\sigma} & = & \left(x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}),\\ \mathrm{MCR}_{2}^{\sigma} & = & \left(x_{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}). \end{eqnarray*}\] Then \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{\sigma} & = & \left(x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}),\\ \mathrm{MCR}_{2}^{\sigma} & = & \left(x_{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}),\\ \mathrm{CR}_{1}^{\sigma} & = & x_{1}\times\left(x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}),\\ \mathrm{CR}_{2}^{\sigma} & = & x_{2}\times\left(x_{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{2}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}), \end{eqnarray*}\]

and \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathrm{PCR}_{1}^{\sigma} & = & \mathrm{CR}_{1}^{\sigma}/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x}),\\ \mathrm{PCR}_{2}^{\sigma} & = & \mathrm{CR}_{2}^{\sigma}/\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}\sigma_{12}\right)/\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x}). \end{eqnarray*}\] Notice that risk decomposition from Euler’s theorem above is the same decomposition we motived at the beginning of the chapter. \(\blacksquare\)

13.2.3.2 Risk decomposition using \(\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\)

Let \(\mathrm{RM}(\mathbf{x)}=\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\). Because \(\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\) is homogenous of degree 1 in \(\mathbf{x},\) by Euler’s theorem \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})&=x_{1}\frac{\partial\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}+x_{2}\frac{\partial\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}+\cdots+x_{n}\frac{\partial\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{n}}\\ &=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}. \end{aligned} \tag{13.10}\] Now, \[\begin{align*} \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}) & =W_{0}\times q_{\alpha}^{R_{p}}(\mathbf{x})=W_{0}\times\left(\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})+\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)\\ & =W_{0}\times\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right), \\ \frac{\partial\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}} & =W_{0}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)\\ & =W_{0}\times\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right). \end{align*}\] Then \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} =W_{0}\times\left(\mu_{i}+\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right), \tag{13.11}\] \[ \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} =x_{i}\times W_{0}\times\left(\mu_{i}+\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right), \tag{13.12}\] \[ \mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} =x_{i}\times W_{0}\times\left(\mu_{i}+\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)/\left(W_{0}\times\left(\mu_{p}(\mathbf{x})+\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)\right). \tag{13.13}\]

It is often common practice to set \(\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{0}\) when computing \(\mathrm{VaR}_{p,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\) especially for short time horizons such as a day. In this case, \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} & = & W_{0}\times\left(\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)\propto\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma},\\ \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} & = & x_{i}\times W_{0}\times\left(\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x)}}\times q_{\alpha}^{Z}\right)\propto\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma},\\ \mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\mathrm{VaR}} & = & x_{i}\times\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x)}}=\mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma}, \end{eqnarray*}\]

and we see that the portfolio normal VaR risk decompositions above give the same information as the portfolio volatility risk decompositions Equation 13.7 - Equation 13.9.

13.2.4 Interpreting marginal contributions to risk

The risk decomposition ?eq-RiskBudgetRiskDecomposition shows that any risk measure that is homogenous of degree one in the portfolio weights can be additively decomposed into a portfolio weighted average of marginal contributions to risk. The marginal contributions to risk Equation 13.1 are the partial derivatives of the risk meausre \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\) with respect to the portfolio weights, and so one may think that they can be interpreted as the change in the risk measure associated with a one unit change in a portfolio weight holding the other portfolio weights fixed: \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\approx\frac{\Delta\sigma_{p}}{\Delta x_{i}}\Rightarrow\Delta\sigma_{p}\approx\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\cdot\Delta x_{i}. \] If the portfolio weights were unconstrained this would be the correct interpretation. However, the portfolio weights are constrained to sum to one, \(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=1\), so the increase in one weight implies an offsetting decrease in the other weights. Hence, the formula \(\Delta\sigma_{p}\approx\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\cdot\Delta x_{i}\) ignores this re-allocation effect.

To properly interpret the marginal contributions to risk, consider the total derivative of \(\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\): \[ \begin{aligned} d\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x}) & =\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}}dx_{1}+\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}}dx_{2}+\cdots+\frac{\partial\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{N}}dx_{N}\\ & =\mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{RM}dx_{1}+\mathrm{MCR}_{2}^{RM}dx_{2}+\cdots+\mathrm{MCR}_{N}^{RM}dx_{N}.\nonumber \end{aligned} \tag{13.14}\] First, consider a small change in \(x_{i}\) offset by an equal change in \(x_{j},\) \(\Delta x_{i}=-\Delta x_{j}\). That is, the small increase in allocation to asset \(i\) is matched by a corresponding decrease in allocation to asset \(j\). From Equation 13.14, the approximate change in the portfolio risk measure is \[ \Delta\mathrm{RM}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\approx\left(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{RM}-\mathrm{MCR}_{j}^{RM}\right)\Delta x_{i}. \tag{13.15}\]

When rebalancing a portfolio, the reallocation does not have to be limited to two assets. Suppose, for example, the reallocation is spread across all of the other assets \(j\neq i\) so that \[ \Delta x_{j}=-\alpha_{j}\Delta x_{i}\text{ s.t. }\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}=1. \] Then \[ \sum_{j\neq i}\Delta x_{j}=-\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}\Delta x=-\Delta x_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}=-\Delta x_{i}, \] and \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta\mathrm{RM}(\mathbf{x}) & \approx\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{RM}\cdot\Delta x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{MCR}_{j}^{RM}\cdot\Delta x_{j}\\ & =\left(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{RM}-\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}\cdot\mathrm{MCR}_{j}^{RM}\right)\Delta x_{i}.\nonumber \end{aligned} \tag{13.16}\]

In matrix notation the result Equation 13.16 be written as \[ \Delta\mathrm{RM}(\mathbf{w}) \approx\left((\mathbf{MCR}^{RM})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\Delta x_{i}, \tag{13.17}\] where \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{MCR}^{RM} & =(\mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{RM},\ldots,\mathrm{MCR}_{n}^{RM})^{\prime}, \\ \boldsymbol{\alpha} & =(-\alpha_{1},\ldots,-\alpha_{i-1},1,-\alpha_{i+1},\ldots-\alpha_{n})^{\prime}. \end{aligned} \]

13.4 Understanding Portfolio Volatility Risk Decompositions

Portfolio volatility risk reports described in the previous sub-section are commonly used in practice. As such, it is important to have a good understanding of the volatility risk decompositions and, in particular, the asset contributions to portfolio volatility. In the portfolio volatility risk decomposition Equation 13.5, the marginal contribution from asset \(i\) given by: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma} & = \text{i-th row of }\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{i}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}. \end{aligned} \tag{13.18}\] The interpretation of this formula, however, is not particularly intuitive. With a little bit of algebra, we can derive two alternative representations of Equation 13.18 that are easier to interpret and give better intuition about the asset contributions to portfolio volatility.

13.4.1 \(\sigma-\rho\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\)

Proposition 13.3 (\(\sigma-\rho\) decomposition) The \(\sigma-\rho\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\) is \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\sigma_{i}\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x}), \tag{13.19}\] where \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{corr}(R,R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))\) is the correlation between the return on asset \(i\) and the return on the portfolio.

Remarks:

  1. Equation Equation 13.19 shows that an asset’s marginal contribution to portfolio volatility depends on two components: (1) the asset’s return volatility, \(\sigma_{i}\) (sometimes called standalone volatility); (2) the asset’s correlation with the portfolio return, \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\).
  2. For a given standalone volatility, \(\sigma_{i},\) the sign and magnitude of \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\) depends on \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\). Because \(-1\leq\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\leq1\), it follows that \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\leq\sigma_{i}\) and \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\sigma_{i}\) only if \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=1\).
  3. If an asset’s return is uncorrelated with all of the returns in the portfolio then \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=\left(x_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)/\sigma_{p}\) and \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\left(x_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)/\sigma_{p}\).

Proposition 13.4 (\(x-\sigma-\rho\) decomposition) Using Equation 13.19 the \(x-\sigma-\rho\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma}=x_{i}\times\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\) is: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma} & = x_{i}\times\sigma_{i}\times\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\\ & = asset\,allocation\times standalone\,volatility\times correlation\,with\,portfolio \end{aligned} \tag{13.20}\]

Remarks:

  1. Equation Equation 13.20 shows that an asset’s contribution to portfolio volatility depends on three components: (1) the asset’s allocation weight \(x_{i}\); (2) the asset’s standalone return volatility, \(\sigma_{i}\); (3) the asset’s correlation with the portfolio return, \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\).
  2. \(x_{i}\times\sigma_{i}=\) standalone contribution to portfolio volatility, which ignores correlation effects with other assets. In the typical situation, \(\rho_{i,p}\neq1\) which implies that \(\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma}<w_{i}\times\sigma_{i}\). Hence, an asset’s contribution to portfolio volatility will almost always be less than its standalone contribution to portfolio volatility.
  3. \(\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma}=x_{i}\times\sigma_{i}\) only when \(\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=1\). That is, an asset’s contribution to portfolio volatility is equal to its standalone contribution only when its return is perfectly correlated with the portfolio return.
  4. If all assets are perfectly correlated (i.e., \(\rho_{ij}=1\) for all \(i\) and \(j\)) then all asset contributions to portfolio volatility are equal their standalone contributions, \(x_{i}\times\sigma_{i}\), and \(\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}\sigma_{1}+x_{2}\sigma_{2}+\cdots+x_{N}\sigma_{N}\). This is the risk decomposition that would occur if there are no diversification effects.

The derivation of Equation 13.19 is straightforward. Recall, the vector of asset marginal contributions to portfolio volatility is given by \[ \frac{\partial\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})} \] Now, \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}=\left(\begin{array}{cccc} \sigma_{1}^{2} & \sigma_{12} & \cdots & \sigma_{1n}\\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_{2}^{2} & \cdots & \sigma_{2n}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ \sigma_{1n} & \sigma_{n2} & \cdots & \sigma_{n}^{2} \end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c} x_{1}\\ x_{2}\\ \vdots\\ x_{n} \end{array}\right) \]

Without loss of generality, consider the first row of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\) \[ x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}+\cdots+x_{n}\sigma_{1n} \] This expression is the covariance between the return on asset 1, \(R_{1}\), and the return on the portfolio, \(R_{p}(\mathbf{x})\): \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathrm{cov}(R_{1},R_{p}(\mathbf{x})) & = & \mathrm{cov}(R_{1},x_{1}R_{1}+x_{2}R_{2}+\cdots+x_{n}R_{n})\\ & = & \mathrm{cov}(R_{1},x_{1}R_{1})+\mathrm{cov}(R_{1},x_{1}R_{1})+\cdots+\mathrm{cov}(R_{1},x_{n}R_{n})\\ & = & x_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{12}+\cdots+x_{n}\sigma_{1n}\\ & = & \sigma_{1,p}(\mathbf{x}) \end{eqnarray*}\] Then we can re-write Equation 13.18 for \(i=1\) as \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{\sigma}=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}\right)_{1}}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\sigma_{1,p}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})} \tag{13.21}\]

Define the correlation between the return on asset 1 and the return on the portfoio as \[ \rho_{1,p}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathrm{corr}(R_{1},R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{1},R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\sigma_{1,p}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})} \tag{13.22}\] Then we can write \[ \sigma_{1,p}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1,p}(\mathbf{x}) \tag{13.23}\] Substituting Equation 13.23 into Equation 13.21 gives the \(\sigma-\rho\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{\sigma}\) \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{1}^{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1,p}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}=\sigma_{1}\rho_{1,p}(\mathbf{x}) \]

13.4.2 \(\sigma-\beta\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\)

Proposition 13.5 (\(\sigma-\beta\) decomposition) The \(\sigma-\beta\) decomposition for \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}\) is \[ \mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}) \tag{13.24}\] where \[ \beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{i},R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\mathrm{var}(R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{i},R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})} \tag{13.25}\]

Here, \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\) is called asset \(i\)’s beta to the portfolio.2 This decomposition follows directly from the \(\sigma-\rho\) decomposition Equation 13.19 since \[ \beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{i},R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{\rho_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{i}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}\Rightarrow\rho_{i,p}(x)=\frac{\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{i}}. \] It follows that: \[ \mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma} = x_{i}\times\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})\times\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}) \tag{13.26}\] \[ \mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma} = x_{i}\times\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x}) \tag{13.27}\]

Remarks:

  • By construction, the beta of the portfolio is \(1\) \[ \beta_{p,p}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_{p}(\mathbf{x}),R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\mathrm{var}(R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}=\frac{\mathrm{var}(R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}{\mathrm{var}(R_{p}(\mathbf{x}))}=1. \]
  • When \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})=1\), \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x}),\mathrm{\,CR}_{i}^{\sigma}=x_{i}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), and \(\mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma}=x_{i}\). In this case, an asset’s marginal contribution to portfolio volatility is portfolio volatility and its percent contribution to portfolio volatility is its allocation weight. Intuitively, when \(\beta_{i,p}(x)=1\) the asset has the same risk, in terms of volatility contribution, as the portfolio.
  • When \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})>1\), \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}>\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), \(\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma}>w_{i}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), and \(\mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma}>x_{i}\). In this case, the asset’s marginal contribution to portfolio volatility is more than portfolio volatility and its percent contribution to portfolio volatility is more than its allocation weight. Intuitively, when \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})>1\) the asset has more risk, in terms of volatility contribution, than the portfolio. That is, having the asset in the portfolio increases the portfolio volatility.
  • When \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})<1\), \(\mathrm{MCR}_{i}^{\sigma}<\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), \(\mathrm{CR}_{i}^{\sigma}<x_{i}\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{x})\), and \(\mathrm{PCR}_{i}^{\sigma}<x_{i}\). In this case, the asset’s marginal contribution to portfolio volatility is less than portfolio volatility and its percent contribution to portfolio volatility is less than its allocation weight. Intuitively, when \(\beta_{i,p}(\mathbf{x})<1\) the asset has less risk, in terms of volatility contribution, than the portfolio. That is, having the asset in the portfolio decreases the portfolio volatility.

13.6.1 Stock and Bond Portfolios

Consider forming portfolios consisting of two assets: (1) bond fund, and (2) stock fund. Let \(R_B\) and \(R_S\) denote the simple per-period returns on the bond and stock funds, respectively, and assume returns are characterized by the GWN model:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_B \sim N(\mu_B, \sigma_B^2), ~ R_S \sim N(\mu_S, \sigma_S^2), \\ \mathrm{cov}(R_B,R_S) =\sigma_{BS}, ~ \mathrm{cor}(R_B,R_S)=\rho_{BS}. \end{aligned} \] Let \(\mathbf{R} = (R_B,R_S)'\). The GWN model in matrix form is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R} & \sim N(\mu, \Sigma) \\ \mu & = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_B \\ \mu_S \end{pmatrix}, ~ \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_B^2 & \sigma_{BS} \\ \sigma_{BS} & \sigma_S^2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

13.6.2 Defining Risk Parity Portfoliofolio

The idea behind risk parity is to use the risk budgeting information for a portfolio to create a long only portfolio with equal risk contributions. To illustrate, consider a two asset stock and bond portfolio with return \(R_p = x_B R_B + X_S R_S\). Using Euler’s Theorem we can decompose portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}'\Sigma \mathbf{x}}\) as:

\[ \sigma_p = x_B \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial x_B} + x_S \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial x_B} = \mathrm{CR}_B + \mathrm{CR}_S. \]

In the risk parity portfolio we find \(x_B>0\) and \(x_S>0\) such that \(\mathrm{CR}_B = \mathrm{CR}_S\) and \(x_B+x_S=1\). To do this we can utilize the \(x-\sigma-\rho\) decomposition of \(\mathrm{CR}_B\) and \(\mathrm{CR}_S\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CR}_B &= x_B \times \sigma_B \times \rho_{Bp}, \\ \mathrm{CR}_S &= x_S \times \sigma_S \times \rho_{Sp}, \\ \rho_{Bp} &= \mathrm{cor}(R_B,R_p)= \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_B,R_p)}{\sigma_B \sigma_p},\\ \rho_{Sp} &= \mathrm{cor}(R_S,R_p)= \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_S,R_p)}{\sigma_S \sigma_p}. \end{aligned} \] Now,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{cov}(R_B,R_p) &= \mathrm{cov}(R_B,x_B R_B + x_S R_S) = \mathrm{cov}(R_B,x_B R_B) + \mathrm{cov}(R_B,x_S R_S) \\ & = x_B \mathrm{cov}(R_B,R_B) + x_S\mathrm{cov}(R_B, R_S) \\ & = x_B \sigma_B^2 + x_S \sigma_{BS}, \\ \mathrm{cov}(R_S,R_p) &= x_S \sigma_S^2 + x_B \sigma_{BS}, \end{aligned} \] so that

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_{Bp} &= \frac{x_B \sigma_B^2 + x_S \sigma_{BS}}{\sigma_B \sigma_p}, \\ \rho_{Sp} &= \frac{x_S \sigma_S^2 + x_B \sigma_{BS}}{\sigma_S \sigma_p}. \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(\mathrm{CR}_B = \mathrm{CR}_S\) then gives: \[ \begin{aligned} x_B \times \sigma_B \frac{x_B \sigma_B^2 + x_S \sigma_{BS}}{\sigma_B \sigma_p} &= x_S \times \sigma_S\frac{x_S \sigma_S^2 + x_B \sigma_{BS}}{\sigma_S \sigma_p} \Rightarrow \\ x_B^2 \sigma_B^2 + x_Bx_S \sigma_{BS} &= x_S^2 \sigma_S^2 + x_Sx_B \sigma_{BS} \Rightarrow \\ x_B^2 \sigma_B^2 &= x_S^2 \sigma_S^2 \Rightarrow \\ x_B \sigma_B &= x_S \sigma_S, \end{aligned} \] where the last line uses the assumption that \(x_B>0\) and \(x_S>0\). Using \(x_S = 1 - x_B\) we can solve for \(x_B\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_B\sigma_B &= (1-x_B)\sigma_S =\sigma_S - x_B\sigma_S \Rightarrow \\ x_B(\sigma_B + \sigma_S) &= \sigma_S \Rightarrow \\ x_B &= \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_B + \sigma_S}. \end{aligned} \] Then \[ x_S = 1 - x_B = \frac{\sigma_B + \sigma_S}{\sigma_B + \sigma_S}- \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_B + \sigma_S} = \frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_B + \sigma_S}. \] Remarks:

  1. In the RP portfolio the amount invested in bonds is proportional to the volatility of stocks, and the amount invested in stocks is proportional to the volatility of bonds. Since stocks are generally more volatile that bonds, this means that the amount invested in bonds will be greater than the amount invested in stocks.
  2. The solution of the RP portfolio does not depend on the correlation between bonds and stocks, \(\rho_{BS}\). We will see in the next section that this result only holds for a two asset portfolio.
  3. Using a little algebra, we can show that in the RP portfolio the amount invested in bonds (stocks) is proportional to the inverse of the volatility of bonds (stocks). To see this, write:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_B &= \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_B + \sigma_S} = \frac{\frac{\sigma_S\sigma_B}{\sigma_B}}{\sigma_B+\sigma_S} \\ &= \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_B}}{\frac{\sigma_B+\sigma_S}{\sigma_B\sigma_S}} = \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_B}}{\frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_B\sigma_S} + \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_B\sigma_S}} \\ &= \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_B}}{\frac{1}{\sigma_S} + \frac{1}{\sigma_B}} = \frac{\sigma_B^{-1}}{\sigma_S^{-1}+\sigma_B^{-1}}, \\ x_S &= \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_S}}{\frac{1}{\sigma_S} + \frac{1}{\sigma_B}} = \frac{\sigma_S^{-1}}{\sigma_S^{-1}+\sigma_B^{-1}}. \end{aligned} \]

  1. With inverse volatility weighting, we see that an asset’s weight is inversely related to its volatility. This means that highly volatile assets like stocks receive small weights, and mildly volatile assets like bonds receive large weights. Notice that a RP portfolio becomes an equally weighted portfolio when \(\sigma_B = \sigma_S\).

  2. Inverse volatility weighting of assets (and then normalized so weights sum to 1) is a common way to form a RP portfolio as it is easy to do (just need estimates of volatility). However, for a portfolio of more than two assets inverse volatility weighting to form a RP portfolio works only when all assets are uncorrelated.

  3. We can also determine the RP portfolio weights using the \(x-\beta-\sigma\) decomposition of \(\mathrm{CR}_B\) and \(\mathrm{CR}_S\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CR}_B &= x_B \beta_{Bp} \sigma_p,\\ \mathrm{CR}_S &= x_S \beta_{Sp} \sigma_p,\\ \beta_{Bp} &= \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_B,R_p)}{\mathrm{var}(R_p)} = \frac{\sigma_{Bp}}{\sigma_p^2},\\ \beta_{Bp} &= \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R_S,R_p)}{\mathrm{var}(R_p)} = \frac{\sigma_{Sp}}{\sigma_p^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CR}_B & = \mathrm{CR}_S \Rightarrow \\ x_B \beta_{Bp} \sigma_p & = x_S \beta_{Sp} \sigma_p \Rightarrow \\ x_B \beta_{Bp} &= x_S \beta_{Sp} = (1 - x_B)\beta_{Sp} \Rightarrow \\ x_B & = \frac{\beta_{Sp}}{\beta_{Sp}+\beta_{Bp}} = \frac{\beta_{Bp}^{-1}}{\beta_{Sp}^{-1}+\beta_{Bp}^{-1}}, \\ x_S & = \frac{\beta_{Bp}}{\beta_{Sp}+\beta_{Bp}} = \frac{\beta_{Sp}^{-1}}{\beta_{Sp}^{-1}+\beta_{Bp}^{-1}}. \end{aligned} \]

Here, we see that in a RP portfolio an asset’s weights is inversely related to its beta to the portfolio. This result holds in general for portfolios with more than two assets. The higher an asset’s beta to the portfolio is, the higher is its contribution to portfolio risk and the smaller is its weight in the RP portfolio. Notice that the RP portfolio is an equally weighted portfolio when both assets have the same beta to the portfolio.

  1. For the RP portfolio, it can be shown that \(\rho_{Bp} = \rho_{Sp}\). That is, both assets have the same correlation with the RP portfolio.
